Day 5: Building Your BYU Experience

What can you gain from opportunities beyond your required courses?

This is the title slide, placed after the review as requested. The throughline: college offers access to people and opportunities while you focus on developing yourself. You cannot do everything. You can learn to notice, ask, try and reflect. About 1 minute.

You have room to explore

College gives you a period focused on learning, with access to people who study and work on questions you may never have encountered.

Some opportunities will change how you think about your direction.

You have other demands on your time. Finding a few worthwhile experiences matters more than doing everything.

Do not imply that all students have abundant free time. Many work, have family responsibilities or commute. The useful distinction is having nearby opportunities and people during a season devoted in part to learning. Keep the tone invitational, not a productivity lecture. About 2 minutes.

Even within Data Science, there is a lot to discover

Methods

Statistics, machine learning, computing, mathematical models, visualization

Questions and settings

Health, sports, science, public policy, business, the arts, and more

Being sure of your major does not mean you already know which parts of the field you will enjoy.

Address the student who has already chosen Data Science. Exploration can happen within a single degree. Avoid asking students to rank their strengths before they have experienced these areas. Ask for one question they are curious about, even if it is tentative. About 2 minutes.

What a course may not show you

An event or experience can introduce you to:

  • a question you had not considered
  • people working on that question
  • a way of doing the work that you might enjoy or want to change

Give a concrete example: a student goes to a research talk about a field they do not know and leaves with a question for the speaker, even though much of the technical content goes over their head. Attending once can be worthwhile. About 1 minute.

Ways to begin

An hour or an afternoon

Forum, research talk, guest speaker, club meeting, student poster session

A longer commitment

Club project, research group, internship, student leadership

Start with a visit or a conversation. You can decide later whether to do more.

Explain that a first visit is a useful experiment. A student can attend a club meeting or hear a talk without committing to an ongoing project. Experiences differ in cost of time and in what they reveal. About 2 minutes.

People you can talk with

  • An advisor about courses, a possible change in direction, or an experience you want to fit into your plans
  • A professor about something from class, their research, or an idea you want to explore

You can go to office hours simply because you are curious.

Day 4 introduced the advisement center. These are examples of things students might discuss, not prescribed scripts or limits on what these people can help with. Connect briefly to the existing advisor and TA lab assignment. Faculty office hours also welcome students with curiosity or incomplete ideas. About 2 minutes.

Being social can be hard. A useful conversation can begin with one sincere question. Do not frame social ease as a prerequisite for belonging or career success. Other people’s experiences can help students spend their limited time well. About 1 minute.

Informational interviews: a reminder

Ask someone about their work and what they have learned.

“What skill matters in your work that job descriptions often leave out? How could a student develop it?”

If advice is useful, try it. Tell the person what happened afterward.

Recall Day 3: the purpose is learning about a person and their work, not asking for a job. Students can follow up with an update when they act on good advice, which can begin an informal mentoring relationship. One thoughtful question is enough to get started. About 2 minutes.

Which opportunities deserve your time?

There is more to try than you can fit into a semester.

Read the description. Ask someone who went. Try one that interests you.

An older student’s view can help you judge what you might learn from an event. Your own experience can change that judgment.

Avoid ranking experiences by prestige. The learning payoff differs by person and moment. Some events are social, some technical, some an introduction to a new question. Free food can get someone through the door, but ask what else the experience might offer. About 1 minute.

Discuss the tradeoffs

In small groups, compare two events.

What might you gain from each? What could make either less worthwhile for you right now?

Would an older student’s experience change your initial choice?

About 3 minutes. Keep discussion grounded in the descriptions, not fabricated reports about event quality. Possible tradeoffs: a campus fair offers breadth and people but can feel crowded; a research conference exposes specific questions but technical detail may be hard to follow; a workshop connects data to a domain but may require more background; a club competition offers practice but takes more time; a forum may broaden perspective without an immediate technical payoff. These are possibilities, not verified reviews. Invite multiple views.

Time spent can have several kinds of value

You discover a preference. You learn what you want more of, or less of.

Debrief the event activity. Disliking a session can still clarify a preference, provided students approach the event with curiosity. These are possible benefits, not guaranteed outcomes of attendance. About 1 minute.
